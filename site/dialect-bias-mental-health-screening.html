Research

Dialect bias in mental health screening. The signal is there. The tool can’t hear it.

What the research says about language tools and the patients they misread, and what building against it requires.

Screening tools and the language models now layered on top of them read words. Most were built on one variety of English, written in fairly direct terms. A patient who says the same thing another way is not being unclear. The tool was never built to hear it.

What the research shows

The pattern holds across the language technology health care is starting to rely on.

  • Depression detection. Language-based depression models were over three times less predictive for Black users than for white users (Penn, PNAS 2024).
  • Speech recognition. Five commercial speech recognition systems made nearly twice as many errors for Black speakers as for white speakers (Koenecke et al., PNAS 2020). Ambient scribes are built on this kind of system.
  • Automated reading of text. Posts written in African American English were more likely to be labeled offensive by widely used classifiers (Sap et al., ACL 2019).
Current tools are least accurate for Black, Brown, LGBTQ+, rural and Medicaid patients.

Why it matters in screening

The failure is rarely dramatic. A real signal reads as ambiguous, scores lower than it should, or is missed. In screening and early detection, that false negative is the costly error: the person who needed someone to follow up never gets flagged.

It is also the error nobody sees. A missed flag leaves no trace in the chart, so a tool can look accurate on average while it fails the patients it reaches least.

How VLAP is built against it

We built VLAP on the patients every other tool gets wrong. That means three things in practice.

  • Measured by language variety. Every result is reported by how patients write, starting with African American Language.
  • Tested against the tool in use today. On held-out data, VLAP scored 0.83 on suicide-attempt severity against 0.65 for MIT’s Suicide Risk Lexicon, and 0.97 F1 on substance use in clinical notes against 0.83 for a keyword rule.
  • Every flag shows its words. VLAP flags a named signal from a taxonomy of 55 signals across seven dimensions, with the exact sentence behind it, so the clinician can check the basis and decide.
